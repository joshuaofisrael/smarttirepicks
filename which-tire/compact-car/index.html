Which tire should I get for a compact or economy car?

Match the door placard first. A cheaper tire in the same size is the right tire only when its load index and speed rating are at least what the vehicle maker lists. After that, the UTQG treadwear number is the federal way to compare tread life on the government course.

Last updated: November 21, 2026

By Smart Tire Picks Editorial Team

This page is for general information only and is not professional, safety, or legal advice. Always confirm tire size, load index, speed rating, and other specs against your vehicle's door placard and owner's manual, and have tires installed by a licensed professional. Chain and traction rules change. Follow the sign posted on the road. We have not lab tested products. See our Disclaimer.

The short answer

For a compact or economy car, buy the size on the door placard, or another size the vehicle maker recommends. Then match the load index and the speed rating. Only after those match should you use the UTQG treadwear number to compare how long the tread should last on the government course. A lower price is not one of those checks.

NHTSA TireWise says all season tires can handle a variety of road conditions and have some mud and snow capability, that winter tires are more effective than all season tires in deep snow, and that summer tires are not designed for temperatures below freezing or for snow and ice. That is the season choice. The grade molded beside it is how you compare two placard correct tires without a road test we did not run.

What the treadwear number is, and is not

49 CFR 575.104 applies to new pneumatic tires for passenger cars. It does not apply to deep tread winter type snow tires, space saver or temporary use spare tires, tires with a nominal rim diameter of 12 inches or less, or limited production tires as that section defines them. A compact car's everyday tires are inside that rule. A winter tire for the same car can be outside it, and a missing number is not a grade of zero.

The shopper text in that rule says a tire graded 150 would wear one and a half times as well on the government course as a tire graded 100. It also says real use can depart from that because of driving habits, service, roads, and climate. Grades come in multiples of 20. NHTSA puts the same idea in one line: the control tire is grade 100, and a grade of 200 should wear twice as long as the control tire.

On the TireWise page read 21 November 2026, NHTSA described the mix of current tires this way. It did not print the day the mix was counted, so this is the mix NHTSA was publishing when we read the page.

  • Treadwear below 200: 15 percent
  • 201 to 300: 25 percent
  • 301 to 400: 32 percent
  • 401 to 500: 20 percent
  • 501 to 600: 6 percent
  • Above 600: 2 percent

Most current tires sit in the 301 to 400 band. A number above 600 is uncommon. Use the band to see whether a cheap tire's number is ordinary. Do not treat the number as a promised mileage. The warranty booklet is a separate document. For how those miles show up on the tire, see how long tires last and tread depth.

Wet stops and heat, which a small tire still has to do

NHTSA says traction grades run from AA down to C, and a higher grade should let a car stop on wet roads in a shorter distance. The same page's current mix was 15 percent AA, 77 percent A, 7 percent B, and only 4 tire lines at C. A budget tire graded B is in a small group. A tire graded C is rarer still, and the federal explanation says a tire marked C may have poor traction.

That explanation, printed in 49 CFR 575.104, limits the test to straight ahead braking on specified government asphalt and concrete. It leaves out acceleration, cornering, hydroplaning, and peak traction. If rain is the reason you are shopping, start with which tire for rain, then use the letter to compare tires that already match the placard.

Temperature grades run A, then B, then C. NHTSA's mix was 62 percent A, 34 percent B, and 4 percent C. The rule says grade C is a level all passenger car tires must meet under Federal Motor Safety Standard No. 109, which is the name printed in the UTQG explanation. A and B are higher on the laboratory wheel than that minimum. The warning says the grade assumes the tire is properly inflated and not overloaded. Excessive speed, underinflation, or excessive loading can cause heat buildup and possible tire failure. In a hot climate, read which tire for a hot climate after the placard is copied.

Load index and speed rating, including the P metric trap

USTMA's care guide says replacement tires should be the original size, or an approved option from the vehicle or tire maker, and never a smaller size or a tire with less load carrying capacity than the original at placard pressure. It recommends that all four tires share size, load index, speed rating, and construction. It says never mix P metric or metric passenger tires with light truck tires on the same vehicle.

The same guide warns that an equal or greater load index does not always mean equal capacity at every inflation pressure, especially when a P metric tire is compared with a metric passenger tire. Compact sizes are sold both ways. Copy the full size and the load index from the placard together. The index number alone is not a shopping key. Details are in the load index and speed rating guide.

If the placard or the owner's manual specifies speed rated tires, USTMA says the replacement must have the same or a higher speed rating to keep the vehicle's speed capability. A lower rating means the car's speed has to stay within that tire, and handling can change. The guide also says a speed rating does not mean the car can be driven safely at that maximum in bad weather, or if the tire is underinflated, overloaded, worn out, damaged, or altered. A compact used for commuting still has this check. The speed letter is not a place to save money.

Season, pressure, and the four tires matching

For a compact that stays above freezing, NHTSA's all season description is the everyday category: a variety of conditions, with some mud and snow capability. Where the car sees deep snow, NHTSA says winter tires are more effective. USTMA's severe snow bulletin, TISB 37, says tires for severe snow use meet a snow grip index of at least 1.10 against the 14 inch standard reference tire, or at least 1.12 against the 16 inch reference tire, on a medium packed snow test. Those tires carry an M+S style mark plus the mountain and snowflake. The care guide calls M+S by itself a mark for limited mud and snow service. If you want one set that carries the snowflake, see snow without winter tires and all weather versus all season.

USTMA prefers winter tires on all four wheels. If they go on the front, they must also go on the rear. That includes a front wheel drive compact. Putting winter tires only on the front is the case the guide tells you to avoid.

Pressure is the maintenance item NHTSA ties to money as well as safety. TireWise says proper inflation can save as much as 11 cents a gallon, that only 19 percent of consumers properly inflate their tires, and that proper inflation can extend tire life by 4,700 miles. The right number is on the door label, not the maximum printed on the sidewall. Check cold, including the spare, at least monthly. Tire pressure monitoring on passenger cars from model year 2008 warns when a tire is significantly underinflated. It is not a substitute for the gauge. See tire pressure.

NHTSA says to replace tires at 2/32 inch, and it describes the penny test: Lincoln's head upside down in the groove, and if you can see the top of the head, replace the tire. The same page says 511 people died in tire related crashes in 2024. That figure is NHTSA's count of fatalities in those crashes. It is not a prediction for any one car.

If the maker recommends rotation, NHTSA's range is every 5,000 to 8,000 miles, or sooner if wear is uneven. Some cars with different front and rear sizes cannot rotate. The owner's manual decides. Replacing two tires with a different construction is the case USTMA would rather you avoid. See mixing tires.

Frequently asked questions

Is a cheaper tire in the same size fine for a compact car?

Only if it matches the placard size, or another size the vehicle maker recommends, and it does not give up load capacity or a required speed rating. NHTSA says buy the original size or a maker recommended size. USTMA says never choose less load carrying capacity than the original at placard pressure. The price is not one of those checks. Use the UTQG treadwear number to compare tread life on the government course.

Does a higher UTQG treadwear number mean more miles on my car?

NHTSA says a grade of 200 should wear twice as long as the grade 100 control tire on the government course. The federal explanation says a grade of 150 would wear one and a half times as well as a grade 100 on that course, and that real miles can depart because of driving, service, roads, and climate. It is not a mileage warranty.

Do compact car tires have to show UTQG grades?

49 CFR 575.104 applies to new pneumatic passenger car tires. It does not apply to deep tread winter type snow tires, space saver or temporary use spare tires, tires with a nominal rim diameter of 12 inches or less, or limited production tires as that section defines them. A winter tire can be legal with no UTQG number.

What traction grade is common?

On the NHTSA TireWise page read 21 November 2026, 77 percent of current tires were traction A and 15 percent were AA. A higher letter should stop the car in a shorter distance on wet pavement. The federal warning says the grade is straight ahead braking and leaves out cornering and hydroplaning.

Can I fit a lower speed rating to save money?

If the placard or the owner's manual specifies speed rated tires, USTMA says the replacement must have the same or a higher speed rating to keep the vehicle's speed capability. A lower rating means the car's speed has to stay within that tire. The rating is not permission to drive at that speed in bad weather.
