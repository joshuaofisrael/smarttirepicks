Tire plug vs patch: which repair is safe?

Short answer: neither a plug alone nor a patch alone is a proper repair. The industry standard is a plug (rubber stem) that fills the hole plus a patch that seals the inside, usually as one combination unit, done with the tire off the wheel. Only tread area punctures up to 1/4 inch (6 mm) qualify.

Short answer: the U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association (USTMA) says a rubber stem, or plug, must fill the puncture and a patch must seal the inner liner, and that a plug only or patch only repair is not acceptable. The Tire Industry Association (TIA) gives the same rule and explains why: a plug does not permanently seal the inner liner, and a patch does not fill the hole, so water can reach and corrode the steel belts.

Your tire maker’s own repair policy can be stricter. Some makers restrict repairs on run flat, high speed rated, or specialty tires.

The repair rules shops should follow

  • Off the wheel first. The tire must be removed from the rim and inspected inside. A nail in the tread can hide damage you cannot see from outside (USTMA, TIA).
  • Tread area only. Repairs are limited to the tread. No repairs in the sidewall, and none where the injury extends into the shoulder or belt edge, including holes that angle toward the shoulder. If there is any doubt, the tire comes out of service (USTMA).
  • 1/4 inch (6 mm) maximum. A puncture larger than 1/4 inch in diameter cannot be repaired (USTMA, TIA). Toyo’s bulletin lists 1/4 inch (6.4 mm).
  • No overlapping repairs. Repairs cannot overlap. TIA adds that injuries directly across from each other also mean the tire must be scrapped.
  • Not on a worn out tire. TIA, Toyo and Continental say do not repair a tire worn to its treadwear indicators or to 2/32 inch tread anywhere. Check yours with our tread depth guide.
  • No fixing a bad old repair. A tire with an existing improper repair must be scrapped, and an inner tube is not a substitute for a proper repair (USTMA, Continental).

When a tire cannot be repaired

  • Any hole or cut in the sidewall or bead area, or a sidewall bulge (see our sidewall bulge guide).
  • Damage in the shoulder or belt edge, or a hole angled toward it.
  • A puncture larger than 1/4 inch (6 mm), or a gash rather than a round hole.
  • A tire worn to 2/32 inch or the treadwear indicators.
  • A tire with an existing improper repair, or a new repair that would overlap an old one.
  • Inside damage found after the tire was driven flat or seriously underinflated. See our TPMS warning light guide for catching slow leaks early.

Is a roadside string plug safe to drive on?

Treat it as a way to get to a shop, not a fix. USTMA says never perform an outside in or on the wheel repair, and TIA says on the wheel repairs like string plugs are not recommended and must be considered temporary. A string plug also means no one has looked inside the tire for hidden damage. Once a shop sees an improper repair, the USTMA rule is that the tire must be scrapped, so ask a shop about a proper plug patch repair as soon as you can rather than waiting.

What about sealant and inflator kits?

Many newer cars ship a sealant and compressor kit instead of a spare. TIA says sealants and emergency inflators that contain sealant are not recommended as long term solutions, for the same reasons as a plug alone. Use the kit to reach a shop within the speed and distance printed on the kit and in your owner’s manual, and tell the technician sealant was used. If your car has a temporary spare instead, see our spare tire (donut) limits guide.

After a proper repair

  1. Ask the shop to confirm the tire was removed from the wheel and repaired with a plug plus inside patch.
  2. Set cold pressure to your door placard value, not the sidewall maximum. See the cold tire pressure guide.
  3. Recheck pressure after a day or two of driving to confirm the repair holds.
  4. Check the tire’s age too. A well repaired but very old tire may still be due for replacement. Use the DOT date code decoder and our how long tires last guide.

FAQ

Is a tire plug or a patch better?

Neither is a proper repair on its own. USTMA says a rubber stem (plug) must fill the puncture and a patch must seal the inner liner, and a plug only or patch only repair is not acceptable. The usual repair is a one piece plug patch combination unit installed with the tire removed from the wheel.

Is it safe to drive on a plugged tire?

An outside in string plug should be treated as temporary. USTMA says never perform an outside in or on the wheel repair, and TIA says on the wheel string plugs must be considered temporary. Drive gently to a shop and have the tire demounted, inspected and properly repaired or replaced.

How big a hole can be repaired in a tire?

Passenger and light truck tire punctures up to 1/4 inch (6 mm) in diameter in the tread area can be considered for repair under USTMA and TIA guidance. Larger holes, cuts, or damage in the shoulder or sidewall mean the tire should be replaced.

Can a sidewall puncture be repaired?

No. USTMA limits repairs to the tread area and says do not repair damage that extends into the shoulder or belt edge. TIA says punctures or damage in the shoulder or sidewall are not repairable.

How many times can a tire be patched?

There is no fixed count in the USTMA guidance, but repairs cannot overlap, and TIA adds that injuries directly across from each other mean the tire must be scrapped. Your tire maker may set stricter limits, so ask the shop to check its policy.

Is tire sealant a permanent fix?

No. TIA says sealants and emergency inflators that contain sealant are not recommended as long term solutions to a flat tire. Use the kit to reach a shop and tell the technician sealant was used.
