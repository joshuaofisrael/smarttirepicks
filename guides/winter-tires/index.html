Winter tires: when to switch and what to buy

Short answer: fit winter tires once daytime temperatures stay around 45°F (7°C) or lower, choose tires with the three peak mountain snowflake (3PMSF) symbol, put them on all four wheels, and check your state’s rules before buying studs.

When to switch: the 45°F (7°C) rule

Temperature matters more than the calendar or the first snowfall. Transport Canada explains that below 7°C (about 45°F) all season and summer tires begin to lose elasticity, which means less traction, while winter tires keep their elasticity and grip at much lower temperatures. The Tire and Rubber Association of Canada gives the same trigger: change over once the temperature consistently stays below 7°C.

  • Fall: book the swap when daytime highs start sitting near 45°F. Cold, dry pavement is part of the reason, not only snow.
  • Spring: swap back once temperatures stay above that point. Soft winter compounds are designed for the cold, so follow your tire maker’s guidance on warm weather use.
  • Plan ahead: shops often get busy after the first storm, so an early appointment is easier than a last minute one.

Reading the sidewall: 3PMSF vs M+S

Two markings cause most of the confusion. They are not the same thing.

MarkingWhat it meansWhat it does not tell you
M+S (also M/S, M&S, MS)Meets the USTMA “snow tire” definition. Common on all season tires.It is not a snow traction test result. CAA says tires marked only M and S are not winter tires.
3PMSF (three peak mountain with a snowflake)The tire reached a minimum snow grip index against a standard reference tire in a packed snow traction test (USTMA TISB 37). USTMA places it next to the M+S marking.The test is on medium packed snow, so it does not rate ice grip, wear, or dry braking.

USTMA describes tires that qualify for the mountain snowflake as having superior snow performance compared with tires that only meet its basic M+S snow tire definition. When you shop, look for the symbol on the sidewall of the exact size you buy, since markings can vary by size within one model line.

The Government of British Columbia notes that 3PMSF tires, which include all weather tires, offer better traction on snow and ice, and recommends them for cold weather driving. For a wider look at trade offs, see all season vs winter and all season vs winter vs summer.

Studded tires: check your state first

Studded tire rules are set by each state, and they differ. Some states allow studs only between set dates, and some restrict them further. Washington is a clear example: studded tires are legal there only from November 1 to March 31, the dates apply to visitors too, and there are no individual or out of state waivers. WSDOT also notes that Washington and Oregon share the March 31 removal date while other states may follow different timelines.

  • Look up the rules on your state DOT or DMV website, and on the sites of any state you will drive through.
  • Studless winter tires with the 3PMSF symbol are an option where studs are restricted.
  • Studs are not the same as chains. In Washington, studded tires do not satisfy a posted chain requirement.

Tread depth for snow

Snow grip depends on tread depth, so a winter tire that is legal for summer roads may already be weak in snow. As one government benchmark, British Columbia requires at least 3.5 mm of tread (about 4/32 inch) for a tire to count as a legal winter tire on its posted routes. Check depth across the full width of each tire before the season starts, and see our tread depth guide for how to measure. Also read the DOT date code, because a stored set ages even when the tread looks fine.

Fit four winter tires, not two

Transport Canada advises installing winter tires in sets of four, ideally with the same type, size, speed rating, and load index on every wheel, and not mixing tread patterns or construction. The Tire and Rubber Association of Canada warns never to fit winter tires on the front axle only, because the extra front grip can make the rear slide out (oversteer).

Before you buy, copy the size, load index, and speed rating from your door placard and confirm the winter tire you choose with your installer. See load index and speed rating for how to read the service description.

Cold weather tire pressure

Tire pressure falls as the air gets colder, so the first cold snap often brings a TPMS warning light. Check cold pressure with a gauge at least monthly, and again after a big temperature drop, and set it to the door placard cold PSI, not the maximum on the sidewall. Expect roughly 1 to 2 psi less for every 10°F drop; the cold weather PSI drop calculator gives a quick range.

Seasonal swap checklist

  1. Watch the forecast. Book the swap when highs settle around 45°F (7°C).
  2. Inspect the winter set for tread depth, cracks, bulges, and age before mounting.
  3. Fit all four, matched in size, type, load index, and speed rating as your installer advises.
  4. Ask about TPMS sensors if the winter set is on its own wheels.
  5. Set cold pressure to the placard value after mounting, and recheck after the first cold week.
  6. Clean and store the off season set properly. See our seasonal tire storage guide.

FAQ

When should I put winter tires on?

Plan the swap once daytime temperatures stay at about 45°F (7°C) or lower, not when the first snow arrives. Transport Canada says all season and summer tires begin to lose elasticity and traction below 7°C, while winter tires keep their grip at much lower temperatures. Booking early also helps, because shops get busy after the first storm.

Is an M+S tire a winter tire?

Not by itself. M+S is a mud and snow marking based on a USTMA definition. The three peak mountain snowflake (3PMSF) symbol is only allowed on tires that pass a packed snow traction test against a reference tire. Canadian motoring association CAA states plainly that tires marked only M and S are not winter tires.

Are all weather tires the same as winter tires?

No. All weather tires are year round tires that also carry the 3PMSF symbol, so they have passed the same snow traction test. Dedicated winter tires are built around cold weather compounds and ice grip and are meant to be swapped off in spring. Where winters bring regular ice, deep snow, or long stretches below freezing, dedicated winter tires are the usual choice.

Can I put winter tires on just the front wheels?

Transport Canada and the Tire and Rubber Association of Canada both advise fitting winter tires in sets of four. Two winter tires on the front only give the front much more grip than the rear, which can make the back of the car slide out (oversteer) when braking or turning.

Are studded tires legal where I live?

It depends on your state, and some states set allowed dates or restrict studs. Washington, for example, allows studded tires only from November 1 to March 31 with no out of state waivers. Check your state DOT or DMV, and every state you plan to drive through, before buying studs. Studless winter tires with the 3PMSF symbol avoid these date limits in states like Washington.

Does the 3PMSF symbol mean a tire is good on ice?

Not directly. The test behind the symbol measures traction on medium packed snow. It does not rate ice grip, so a tire can carry the symbol and still be weaker on ice than a dedicated winter tire. Read the maker's own description of ice performance for the exact model.
