Punctured tire: how a mechanic decides to repair or replace

Diagnose a leak, check USTMA repair limits (tread area only, 1/4 inch maximum, tire off the wheel), and know when damage or age means replacement.

Last updated: November 10, 2026

By Smart Tire Picks Editorial Team

This page is for general information only and is not professional, safety, or legal advice. Always confirm tire size, load index, speed rating, and other specs against your vehicle’s door placard and owner’s manual, and have tires installed by a licensed professional. See our Disclaimer.

Diagnose before you plug

A slow leak does not automatically mean a repair. Ask when the pressure warning appeared, whether the car was driven while low, and whether there was a pothole or curb hit; NHTSA notes a tire can lose pressure suddenly after one. Then take the tire off the wheel. USTMA requires the tire to be removed from the rim and inspected for all possible damage, including the inner liner, before any repair is considered. Also check the valve and wheel while it is apart, since a leak is not always in the tread. Pressure holding after inflation is not proof the tire is sound.

This page covers the diagnosis and decision. For the limits themselves in bay terms see tire repair or replace for shops, and for the consumer explanation see tire plug vs patch.

USTMA repair limits

  • Repair is only for the tread area, and the puncture injury must be no greater than 1/4 inch (6 mm) in diameter.
  • Do not repair where the injury reaches the shoulder or belt edge area, or runs at an angle into the shoulder. If there is any question, take the tire out of service.
  • Repairs cannot overlap, and a tire with an existing improper repair must be scrapped.
  • A rubber stem fills the injury and a patch seals the innerliner. A plug alone is unacceptable, and outside in or on the wheel repairs are never to be done.
  • Maker limits can be stricter, for example on run flat tires.

When damage points to replacement

Continental says a tire is beyond repair when the tread is below the 1.6 mm legal limit (a figure that applies in Europe; in the US NHTSA says replace at 2/32 inch), the rubber is cracked, worn or deteriorated, the puncture is 6 mm or larger, the puncture is in the sidewall, or the structure is compromised or has run flat damage. Sidewall, shoulder or internal cord damage means replacement. For bulges after an impact see our sidewall bulge guide.

Age matters too. NHTSA says aging cannot be seen by looking, and that some vehicle and tire manufacturers recommend replacing tires at six to 10 years regardless of treadwear. Check the DOT date code and the maker's policy, and see when to replace tires.

Finish the job and record it

Follow the repair unit maker's instructions, then set pressure to the vehicle manufacturer's cold figure from the placard or owner's manual, not the number on the tire (NHTSA). See how to read the placard and TPMS if the warning light stays on. Note the injury location, size and your decision on the work order, particularly if the customer declines a replacement, so the next shop is not guessing.

Frequently asked questions

Can a nail hole anywhere in the tread be patched?

Not necessarily. USTMA says repairs are limited to the tread area, the injury cannot be greater than 1/4 inch (6 mm) in diameter, and an injury that reaches the shoulder or belt edge area, or runs at an angle into the shoulder, must not be repaired. If there is any question, the tire must be taken out of service.

Is a string plug an adequate permanent repair?

No. USTMA says a rubber stem (plug) must fill the puncture and a patch must seal the innerliner, and that a plug alone is an unacceptable repair. It also says never to perform an outside in or on the wheel repair.

Can a sidewall cut be repaired?

No. Continental says any damage to the sidewall, shoulder or internal cords makes a tire irreparable and requires replacement. Our sidewall bulge guide covers what to do with impact damage.

What if a tire held air after being driven flat?

Holding pressure is not enough. USTMA requires the tire to be removed from the wheel and inspected for all damage, including the inner liner. Continental lists run flat damage and compromised structure as reasons a tire is beyond repair.

Does a run flat tire always qualify for repair?

No. USTMA says some manufacturers set repair limits or restrictions for tires such as run flats, so check the maker's repair policy for the specific tire.

Which pressure should be set after a repair, the number on the sidewall?

No. NHTSA says the correct pressure is the one the vehicle manufacturer lists on the Tire and Loading Information Label or in the owner's manual, not the figure on the tire, and it should be measured cold.

When does age mean replacement?

NHTSA says some vehicle and tire manufacturers recommend replacing tires that are six to 10 years old regardless of treadwear, and that you cannot detect aging just by looking. Check the DOT code and the maker's policy.
