Nitrogen vs air in tires: is nitrogen worth it?

Short answer: for normal cars and light trucks, usually not. The air you already use is about 78 percent nitrogen. A nitrogen fill does leak out a little more slowly, but in Consumer Reports’ year long test the difference was only 1.3 psi over twelve months. Checking your pressure monthly, with either gas, matters far more, and topping up a nitrogen filled tire with regular air is fine.

What is nitrogen tire inflation?

Shops that offer nitrogen run compressed air through a generator that strips out most of the oxygen and moisture, then fill your tires with what is left: mostly nitrogen. The U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association (USTMA) describes it as basically nothing more than dry air with the oxygen removed, and notes that ordinary air already contains about 78 percent nitrogen. So a nitrogen fill is not a different substance in your tires; it is a higher concentration of the gas that is already the main ingredient.

Nitrogen has real uses in demanding jobs. USTMA says aircraft, mining and heavy commercial equipment use it to reduce fire risk if brakes or wheels overheat, and racing teams use dry nitrogen to cut pressure swings caused by moisture. For normal passenger and light truck service, USTMA says nitrogen inflation is not necessary, though it is permissible.

What the tests actually found

  • Consumer Reports, one year test. Consumer Reports filled one tire of each of 31 all season tire models with air and one with 95 percent nitrogen, set both to 30 psi, left them outdoors for a year and rechecked them. Air filled tires lost an average of 3.5 psi and nitrogen filled tires 2.2 psi, a 1.3 psi difference over the whole year. Its tire program manager stresses that both kinds lose pressure, so owners still need to check routinely.
  • NHTSA laboratory study (DOT HS 811 094). In a 90 day static test, new tires inflated with nitrogen lost pressure at roughly two thirds the rate of new tires inflated with air, with similar results under load on a roadwheel. Nitrogen had no direct effect on lab rolling resistance. NHTSA also found that air filled tires in everyday service drift toward more nitrogen on their own: oxygen escapes faster, and oxygen in 76 in service tires had dropped from 21 percent to an average of 15 percent after about three years. That means nitrogen’s advantage shrinks over time.
  • Tire aging. NHTSA’s oven aging test suggested oxygen speeds internal aging, but every tire filled with either nitrogen or normal air passed the durability test. Consumer Reports’ reading is that this is not a practical concern for most drivers, who wear tires out long before oxidation becomes the problem. For age limits see our how long do tires last guide.
  • Other leaks matter more. USTMA points out that punctures, the bead where the tire meets the rim, the valve and the wheel itself can all leak and may cancel out any benefit from nitrogen. Temperature matters too: pressure drops as it gets colder, whatever is inside. See our cold weather tire pressure section.

Industry view, clearly labeled: the Get Nitrogen Institute, an organization that promotes nitrogen inflation to drivers, truckers and fleets, says nitrogen improves handling, fuel efficiency and tire life through better pressure retention and cooler running tires. Those claims come from an advocacy group; the independent findings above show the measurable benefit for passenger tires is small.

Can you mix nitrogen and air?

Yes. USTMA says nitrogen and air can be mixed in any proportion, and that nitrogen filled tires can and should have air added whenever nitrogen is not readily available. Driving on low tires to avoid "diluting" the nitrogen is the real mistake: USTMA warns that relying on nitrogen to reduce pressure checks can lead to underinflated driving and premature tire failure. Consumer Reports agrees there is no harm in adding regular air; it just reduces the nitrogen benefit you paid for.

What do green valve caps mean?

Green valve stem caps are the common signal that a shop or dealer filled the tires with nitrogen, and Consumer Reports notes some cars are sold with them already fitted. The cap itself does nothing special; it is a reminder. If you bought a car with green caps, you can keep topping up with nitrogen where it is convenient or simply use air. Either way, keep a cap on each valve to keep dirt and moisture out of the valve core.

Nitrogen and your TPMS light

Nitrogen does not change how your tire pressure monitoring system works. NHTSA says TPMS only warns when a tire is significantly underinflated and is not a substitute for a monthly check with an accurate gauge. A slower leak from nitrogen just means the light may take a little longer to come on; it does not mean the tire is at the right pressure. Our TPMS warning light guide explains what the solid and flashing lights mean.

Is nitrogen worth paying for?

  • If it is free with new tires: fine to accept. It will not hurt anything.
  • If it costs extra: most drivers are better off spending the money on a good tire gauge. Consumer Reports concludes consumers would save money by using regular air and checking monthly.
  • Either way: follow NHTSA’s advice to check all tires, including the spare, at least once a month when cold, meaning the car has not been driven for at least three hours, and set them to the placard value, not the number on the sidewall. Our cold tire pressure guide walks through it.

FAQ

Is nitrogen better than air for car tires?

Only slightly. In Consumer Reports' one year test nitrogen filled tires lost 2.2 psi versus 3.5 psi for air, a 1.3 psi difference. USTMA says nitrogen is not necessary for normal passenger and light truck tires, and both need monthly pressure checks.

Can I put regular air in tires filled with nitrogen?

Yes. USTMA says nitrogen and air can be mixed in any proportion and that nitrogen filled tires should have air added whenever nitrogen is not readily available. Driving on low tires is the real risk; air only dilutes the nitrogen benefit.

What do green valve caps on tires mean?

Green valve caps usually signal that a dealer or shop filled the tires with nitrogen. The cap itself does nothing special. You can keep using nitrogen or switch to regular air; either way, check pressure monthly.

Does nitrogen improve gas mileage?

Not directly. NHTSA's lab study found nitrogen had no direct effect on rolling resistance. Any fuel saving comes only from tires staying closer to the correct pressure, which regular monthly checks with air also achieve.

How much does nitrogen for tires cost?

Consumer Reports says dealerships and tire shops often charge $5 or more per tire, and some shops include it with new tires. Regular air is usually free or low cost.

Do I still need to check tire pressure with nitrogen?

Yes. USTMA and Consumer Reports both say nitrogen is not a replacement for regular pressure maintenance. NHTSA recommends checking all tires, including the spare, at least monthly when cold, and notes TPMS only warns when a tire is significantly underinflated.
