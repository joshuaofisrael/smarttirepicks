Tire balancing vs wheel alignment: which do you need?

Short answer: get your tires balanced if you feel a vibration that comes and goes with speed, and get a wheel alignment if the car pulls to one side, the steering wheel sits off center, or the tread wears on one edge. Balancing fixes how the tire and wheel spin; alignment fixes the angle the wheels point.

Last updated: October 27, 2026

By Smart Tire Picks Editorial Team

The difference in one table

Tire balancingWheel alignment
What it fixesUneven weight around the tire and wheelWheel angles: camber, caster and toe
Main symptomVibration or shake at certain speedsPulling, off center steering wheel, edge wear
How it is doneWheel spun on a balancer; small weights added to the rimSuspension and steering adjusted to the maker's specs on an alignment rack
Works onEach tire and wheel assemblyThe vehicle's suspension and steering
Typical timesNew tires, remounting, flat repairs, any new vibrationNew tires, after a pothole or curb hit, suspension work, any pull or edge wear

NHTSA's tire safety brochure sums it up: balancing places weights to counter heavy spots and prevent vibration, while alignment sets the wheel angles so the car does not veer and the tires last longer. Both need special equipment and a qualified technician.

Check these first, for free

  1. Tire pressure. Set every tire to the cold pressure on your door placard. A low tire can make a car pull. See our tire pressure guide.
  2. Look at the tread. Edge wear on one side hints at alignment; wear on both shoulders hints at low pressure; wear in the center hints at overinflation. Our tread depth guide shows how to measure.
  3. Look for damage. A bulge, a missing wheel weight or a bent rim lip can all cause shaking. See sidewall bulges.
  4. Note when it happens. Only at certain speeds, only when braking, or all the time. That detail helps a shop find the cause quickly.

Where rotation fits in

Rotation moves tires between positions so they wear evenly; it does not change balance or wheel angles. Many shops check balance when they rotate, and rotating on schedule can make alignment wear easier to spot early. See our tire rotation patterns, and if you are replacing only some tires, read mixing tires first.

Prices vary by shop, region and vehicle, so ask for a quote for each service separately. Some shops include balancing with tire installation.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between tire balancing and wheel alignment?

Balancing evens out the weight around a tire and wheel so it spins smoothly; a technician adds small weights to the rim to offset heavy spots. Alignment adjusts the angles of the wheels (camber, caster and toe) relative to the vehicle so the tires point straight and sit flat on the road. Balancing is about the wheel assembly; alignment is about the car's suspension and steering.

How do I know if I need balancing or alignment?

Vibration in the steering wheel, seat or floor that shows up in a certain highway speed range usually points to imbalance. A car that pulls to one side on a straight, level road, a steering wheel that sits off center when driving straight, or wear on the inside or outside edge of the tread usually points to alignment. Check that all tires are at the placard pressure first, since uneven pressure can also cause a pull.

Should I get my tires balanced when I buy new tires?

Yes. New tires are normally balanced when they are mounted on the wheels, because no tire and wheel is perfectly uniform. Tires are also commonly rebalanced when they are remounted, after a flat repair that removes the tire from the rim, or whenever a vibration appears.

When should I get a wheel alignment?

Consider an alignment when you fit new tires, after hitting a large pothole or curb, after suspension or steering work, or whenever you notice pulling, an off center steering wheel or uneven edge wear. Your owner's manual may also list a check interval.

Can bad alignment cause vibration?

Alignment problems mainly cause pulling and uneven wear rather than a speed dependent vibration. If a vibration remains after a proper balance, the cause may be a bent rim, an out of round tire, tire uniformity issues, worn suspension parts or brakes, so have a technician inspect the car.

Do I need both balancing and alignment?

Often both are worth checking, because they solve different problems. Michelin notes a car can be perfectly aligned and still vibrate from imbalance, and a balanced wheel can still wear unevenly if the alignment angles are wrong.

If my car pulls only when braking, is it alignment?

Probably not. A pull that happens only when you brake usually points to the braking system, not alignment, so have the brakes inspected.
