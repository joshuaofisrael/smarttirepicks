All-season vs winter tires

All-season tires may be enough for mild winters with rare ice and light snow. Winter tires are often considered where sub-freezing temperatures and packed snow or ice are regular, at the cost of a second set, storage, and seasonal swaps.

Core difference

All-season tires aim for acceptable performance across a wide range of conditions. Winter tires prioritize cold-temperature flexibility and snow/ice traction design. Neither category removes the need for appropriate speeds, pressures, and maintenance.

When all-seasons may be enough

  • Mild winters with rare ice and light snow
  • Preference for a single mounted set and simpler logistics
  • Vehicles that see mostly clear paved roads

When winter tires are often considered

  • Regular sub-freezing temperatures and packed snow or ice
  • Hills, rural roads, or early/late season storms
  • Willingness to store a second set and schedule seasonal swaps

Cost and logistics

A winter set adds tire (and sometimes wheel) cost plus storage and mount/balance cycles. Some drivers keep winters on dedicated wheels to simplify swaps and preserve TPMS sensors — discuss options with your installer.

Symbols and labels

Look for the three-peak mountain snowflake (3PMSF) on tires marketed for severe snow service. M+S alone is a mud-and-snow designation with a different meaning — read manufacturer explanations for your model.

The 45°F rule of thumb

Michelin's tire buying guide says winter tire compounds stay flexible below about 45°F (7°C), the point where it says summer and all-season compounds start to harden and lose grip. It suggests switching to winter tires when average daily temperatures fall below that level and switching back once temperatures stay above it. Treat this as a manufacturer guideline, not a fixed law; your local roads and storms matter as much as the thermometer.

The middle option: 3PMSF all-season or all-weather tires

Some all-season tires carry the 3PMSF mark, and many shops call these all-weather tires. Michelin describes them as a middle ground for drivers who want tested severe snow traction without managing two seasonal sets. They are still a compromise: a dedicated winter tire is built only for cold conditions, while a 3PMSF all-season also has to work in summer heat. Example on this site: Michelin CrossClimate 2 overview.

Fit four, not two

Michelin recommends installing winter tires on all four wheel positions. Its FAQ warns that mixing winter tires on one axle with non winter tires on the other can upset vehicle balance, causing understeer or oversteer depending on which axle has less grip. AWD or 4WD helps you get moving, but Michelin notes it does not replace winter tires for braking and cornering.

Quick decision table

Your wintersCommon approach

FAQ

Do winter tires wear out faster?

Michelin says winter tires wear at a comparable rate when used in cold months, but wear faster if left on through warm weather, which is one reason to swap them off in spring.

Why don't winter tires have UTQG treadwear grades?

According to Michelin's FAQ, winter tires are not assigned UTQG treadwear, traction or temperature grades. See our UTQG guide for how grades work on other tires.
